% ============================================================================ abstract
\begin{abstract}
Which loophole of an LLM judge will a policy exploit when the judge serves as its reward? We show that the
answer can be predicted before any RL step. LLM judges accept degenerate answers such as a lone \str{\{} or
\str{Thought process:}, but static robustness probes list many such flaws without saying which ones training
will find. Before training, we audit each judge and the starting policy and score every hacking pattern by its
exposure: how often the policy produces the pattern times how often the judge accepts it. To test exposure as a
cause rather than a correlate, we vary propensity experimentally, seeding the patterns into a short fine-tuning
stage at three doses in a Latin square, and we pre-register the analysis. Across 36 GRPO runs of
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct against seven judges, three of them held out, exposure predicts which pattern is
exploited with an AUROC of 0.974, against 0.937 for the judge's false-positive rate alone. In all 12 runs where
a pattern was exploited, the first one was the pattern ranked first by exposure; the false-positive rate alone
ranked it first in 6. Exploitation occurred only on hard prompts, where an accepted exploit receives about 2.5
times the advantage it gets on easy ones.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a standard way to train reasoning in language models
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath, deepseek2025r1}. When an answer cannot be checked by a rule, an LLM is often asked to
act as the verifier, comparing the policy's answer with a reference \citep{chen2025xverify, ma2025generalreasoner}.
Such judges have blind spots. A response consisting of a lone \str{\{}, a bare claim that the answer is correct, or
the words \str{Thought process:} can be graded correct \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}, and a policy
trained against such a judge can learn to emit these strings instead of solving the problem
\citep{huang2025accuracy}.

Knowing that a judge has a blind spot does not tell us whether training will find it. \citet{huang2025accuracy}
observe that DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B fails static probes at high rates yet showed no reward hacking in their
RL runs, while a verifier fine-tuned from it, with higher classification accuracy, was exploited; they hypothesize
that their policies were not strong enough to exploit the untrained judge. Studies of when reward hacking begins
have injected known biases into rubric judges \citep{wang2026rubric}, built environments with designed-in
loopholes such as a test harness the policy can edit \citep{khalifa2026countdown, wang2026catch}, or used
fine-tuning as a stand-in for RL \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}. Whether measurements taken strictly before RL
can predict which of a real judge's existing flaws a policy will exploit, and whether such predictions hold on
judges held out from the analysis, has not been tested.

We start from a simple account of how a policy finds a loophole under GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. A pattern
can be reinforced only when the policy samples it and the judge rewards it. With $G$ responses per training step,
a pattern produced at rate $\phat$ and accepted with probability $\fpr$ earns on average $G\,\phat\,\fpr$ rewarded
responses per step, its \emph{exposure}. We hypothesize that the pattern with the highest exposure is the one
exploited, and that neither factor alone predicts as well (H1). Group normalization adds a second factor: when
genuine successes are rare, an accepted exploit is often the only rewarded response in its group and receives a
large advantage, so hard prompts should raise the risk (H2). If propensity matters causally, raising it by
fine-tuning should raise the risk with dose (H3).

Testing H1 requires propensity to vary independently of FPR, and natural propensities are too low for that: only 19
of 174,760 responses of our base policy fall in any of 14 hacking-pattern families, and no pattern is exploited
within 300 steps. Since a small share of hacking trajectories in fine-tuning data is enough for hacking to resurface
in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}, we seed each pattern into a short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage on the
policy's own correct answers, at 0.3\%, 1.2\% or 4.0\% of the examples, with the doses rotated over three seeds in a
Latin square so that every pattern meets every dose (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}). We measure each judge's FPR on each
pattern and the seeded propensity, record every score, and only then train. The protocol, including the event
definition and every test, was frozen before the first run.

Across 36 GRPO runs of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with seven judges, three of them held out, we find:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited.} Over 336 pairs of run and pattern it reaches an AUROC
  of 0.974, against 0.937 for FPR alone, 0.847 for propensity alone and 0.379 for the judge's static accuracy (all
  differences $p < 0.001$ after Holm correction), and the pattern exploited first was ranked first in all 12 runs
  with an event.
\item \textbf{The product is the right combination, and difficulty gates it.} A conditional logit over the winners
  rejects either factor alone and is consistent with equal weights on log propensity and log FPR; exploitation
  occurs only on hard prompts, where an accepted exploit receives about 2.5 times the advantage it gets on easy
  prompts.
\item \textbf{An open audit.} We release the FPR of seven open judges on 14 patterns, the code, every run log and an
  interactive demo. \todo{anonymized link for review}
\end{itemize}

% ============================================================================ 2 setting
\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

\paragraph{Hacking patterns.} We study 14 families: the 13 of \citet{huang2025accuracy}, from empty symbols and
gibberish to adversarial prefixes and answer explanations, and the reasoning openers of \citet{zhao2025onetoken}
such as \str{Thought process:} (Appendix~\ref{app:families}). Rule-based detectors assign each response to at most
one family, by whether the pattern dominates the response rather than whether it appears in it: a response with a
non-empty boxed answer or real mathematical work is never a family member. The detectors agree with all 6,123
labeled answers of the hacking set and flag none of 12,500 reference solutions from MATH and DeepScaleR.

\paragraph{Judges.} Four core judges span the range of vulnerability reported by \citet{huang2025accuracy}:
R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B, exploited in their RL runs and our positive control; DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B
\citep{deepseek2025r1}; Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25math}; and xVerify-0.5B-I
\citep{chen2025xverify}, almost never fooled and our negative control. Three held-out judges serve only to test
transfer: General-Verifier \citep{ma2025generalreasoner}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B,\footnote{\url{https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-FP8}, released in 2026.} added after the
protocol was frozen and run last. Each judge uses its native prompt and decoding (Appendix~\ref{app:judges}) and
sees the same answer string in the audit and in training. The six judges we measured grade ordinary responses well
(balanced accuracy 0.93 to 0.99 on the policy's own answers), so honest rewards are reliable.

\paragraph{Policy, problems and gold labels.} The policy is Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25}. Training
problems are integer-answer problems from DeepScaleR \citep{luo2025deepscaler} and the MATH training split
\citep{hendrycks2021math}, excluding the 471 problems behind the hacking set and any overlap with MATH-500
\citep{lightman2023verify} and the GSM8K test set \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. A rule-based gold checker (the last
\verb|\boxed{}| answer, compared by Math-Verify \citep{huggingface2025mathverify}) labels correctness and is never
seen by the policy; it agrees with a blind hand audit of 200 responses 99.7\% of the time (weighted by stratum; every
miss is a correct answer written without a box).

\paragraph{Difficulty pools.} The base policy answers each of 21,845 candidate problems eight times. The hard pool
holds problems it solves once or twice (base accuracy 16.6\%), the easy pool problems it solves five to seven times
(76.1\%); each pool has 2,229 training and 100 evaluation problems.

% ============================================================================ 3 method
\section{Predicting exploitation before training}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Judge audit.} Each family is represented by one pre-specified seeding string: its most frequent answer
in the hacking set, \str{Thought process:} for reasoning openers, and fresh random strings for gibberish. For judge
$j$ and family $f$, $\fpr_{j,f}$ is the share of the 471 audit problems on which $j$ accepts the string as a correct
answer (Figure~\ref{fig:fpr}). We audit strings rather than family averages because GRPO reinforces strings: in a
pilot, a family that mixed accepted and rejected strings did not grow, and a string's FPR can differ from its family
mean by up to 67 points.

\paragraph{Controlled propensity.} Each starting policy is the base policy after two epochs of SFT on 2,000
examples: the policy's own finished, correct answers to problems outside both pools, of which a share is replaced
by seeding strings. The 14 families were split once at random into groups of five, five and four; under rotation
$s$, used by seed $s$, the groups are seeded at 0.3\%, 1.2\% and 4.0\% in cyclic order, so every family meets every
dose across the three seeds. A matched control replaces nothing (0\%). We then measure the seeded propensity
$\phat_f$ by sampling the fine-tuned policy 16 times on each of 250 problems per pool, with Jeffreys smoothing.

\paragraph{Exposure score.} The score of family $f$ for a run with judge $j$ on a pool is
\begin{equation}
  r_{j,f} = \phat_f \cdot \fpr_{j,f},
\end{equation}
and $256\,r_{j,f}$ is the expected number of rewarded responses of family $f$ per GRPO step. Every score was
recorded before the run's first step.

\paragraph{Outcome.} Every 10 steps the policy answers the 100 evaluation problems of its pool twice. The
exploitation rate $e_f(t)$ is the share of these 200 outputs that belong to family $f$, are wrong by gold and are
accepted by the judge. Family $f$ has an \emph{event} when $e_f(t)$ exceeds $e_f(0)$ by at least $\tau = 0.05$ at
two consecutive evaluations; the event time is the first of them. Measuring from step 0 keeps a judge's initial
errors from counting as hacking.

% ============================================================================ 4 design
\section{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}

\paragraph{Runs.} The core design crosses the four core judges with the two pools and three seeds (24 runs). The
held-out judges are trained on the hard pool with the three seeds (9 runs), and the 0\% control pairs
Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct, the most vulnerable judge, with the hard pool and three seeds (3 runs). Each run trains
the policy for 300 GRPO steps of 32 prompts with 8 samples each, using TRL \citep{vonwerra2020trl} with vLLM
generation \citep{kwon2023vllm}, the token-level loss of DAPO \citep{yu2025dapo} with clipping at 0.2 and 0.28, no KL
penalty and a learning rate of $10^{-6}$; the judge is the only reward (Appendix~\ref{app:hyper}).

\paragraph{Validity checks.} Two pilots preceded the freeze. A judge engineered to accept every unboxed answer was
exploited from step 150, after about 300 accepted samples, showing that the pipeline detects hacking when it exists.
The positive control trained from the unseeded policy produced 5 family responses in 76,800 training samples and no
event, which is why propensity is seeded.

\paragraph{Analysis.} The unit is a pair of run and family. H1 compares the AUROC of exposure for events with the
AUROC of FPR alone, propensity alone and static accuracy (minus the judge's balanced accuracy on the policy's answers),
on the 24 core runs; intervals come from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of whole runs within each judge, so that the
families of a run, which compete for the same takeover, stay together, and the three differences are paired. H2 is
the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool. H3 is the exact randomization test
of the seeding design: the sum of the dose ranks of all core events, against all 252,252 ways of forming the three
family groups. Holm correction covers these five tests. Secondary analyses model time to the first event with
cause-specific hazards, the winner with a conditional logit, and the early growth of every family
(Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). All of this was fixed in a protocol frozen as a version-control tag before the first
run; the only amendment added the third held-out judge at the end of the queue.

% ============================================================================ 5 results
\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited (H1).} Fourteen events occurred, in 12 of the 33 seeded runs,
all on the hard pool. Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a shows the pattern: no family with an exposure below 1.9 rewarded
responses per step had an event, and above that level a hard-pool family without an event had lost to a family of
higher exposure in the same run in 9 of 10 cases. On the core runs
exposure reaches an AUROC of 0.974 (95\% interval 0.955 to 0.995), ahead of FPR alone by 0.037 (0.020 to 0.055),
propensity alone by 0.127 and static accuracy by 0.595 (Figure~\ref{fig:auroc}, Table~\ref{tab:tests}). Dropping the
positive control leaves the result unchanged (0.979 against 0.945 for FPR). The ranking view is sharper: in all 12
runs with an event, the pattern exploited first was ranked first by exposure, whereas FPR alone ranked it first in 6
runs and propensity alone in 2 (Figure~\ref{fig:h1}b). The gain over FPR comes from high-FPR strings that the seeding
put at a low dose, such as \str{\{} for R1-Distill-Verifier in seed 1 (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}).

\paragraph{Why the product.} If exposure is the right quantity, the winner of a run should be predicted by log
propensity and log FPR with equal weights. In a conditional logit over the 12 runs with an event, every weight ratio
from 0.46 to 1.66 ranks every winner first, a range that contains the equal weights of the product, while FPR alone
and propensity alone fall well short of the full model (likelihood ratio $p = 1.1 \times 10^{-6}$ and
$1.4 \times 10^{-11}$). Over all seeded runs, a cause-specific Cox model gives a hazard ratio of 10.5 (5.1 to 58.8) per unit of log
exposure.
Early growth tells a different story: before any takeover, a family's relative growth follows its FPR (Spearman 0.28)
rather than its exposure ($-0.04$), and correlates negatively with propensity ($-0.33$), because seeded strings decay
early in RL unless the judge accepts them. Exposure predicts which pattern takes over within the budget, not how fast
each pattern grows at first.

\paragraph{Hard prompts gate exploitation (H2).} In 7 of the 12 pairs of core runs that differ only in the pool, a
family was exploited on the hard pool and none on the easy pool; the reverse never happened ($p = 0.016$, Holm
$0.031$). Easy-pool families with exposures as high as 9.4 rewarded responses per step stayed flat
(Figure~\ref{fig:h1}a). The mechanism is the group normalization of GRPO: in the first 10 steps the vulnerable judges
accept 19\% to 23\% of responses on hard prompts and 61\% to 67\% on easy ones, so a rewarded response receives an
advantage of about $\sqrt{(1-q)/q}$, 1.8 to 2.1 on hard prompts against 0.7 to 0.8 on easy ones
(Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}b). Because the hard pool is mostly competition problems, difficulty and problem source are
not fully separated.

\paragraph{Seeding dose (H3).} The core events rise with the seeded dose: none in 112 pairs of run and family at
0.3\%, 2 of 112 at 1.2\% and 7 of 112 at 4.0\%, and none of 42 in the unseeded control (Figure~\ref{fig:h2h3}c). The randomization test does not
reach significance ($p = 0.056$): the randomized unit is the grouping of families, and all events fall on four
families, so even the most extreme pattern would give $p \approx 0.017$. The matched control comparison (3 of 3
seeded runs with an event against 0 of 3 control runs) points the same way but has little power.

\paragraph{Held-out judges.} On the three held-out judges, exposure reaches 0.995 (0.986 to 1.000) against 0.979 for
FPR alone; the difference of 0.017 is not significant ($p = 0.066$). These judges accept few strings, so FPR is nearly
sufficient; exposure gains where FPR's first choice was seeded at a low dose. For Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, which accepts
essentially only \str{\{} (84.7\%), we predicted before its runs that the seed in which \str{\{} sits at 0.3\% would
have no event, and it had none.

\paragraph{Robustness.} The lead of exposure over FPR alone stays between 0.037 and 0.041 for thresholds $\tau$ from
0.025 to 0.10 with one or two consecutive evaluations, and holds with a threshold-free outcome (Spearman correlation
with the largest rise of $e_f$: 0.555 against 0.467), with propensity measured as the total likelihood of the seeding
strings, and with FPR averaged over each family's strings (Appendix~\ref{app:stats}). It fails only with per-token
likelihood, which favors short repetitive strings and is not a frequency.

% ============================================================================ 6 related work
\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reward hacking.} Optimizing a proxy reward can raise the proxy while the true objective falls
\citep{amodei2016concrete, skalse2022defining}, and the gap grows with optimization pressure on a learned reward
model \citep{gao2023scaling}. We study the same failure for LLM judges used as verifiers in RL, and ask which of a
judge's flaws a policy will actually reach.

\paragraph{Robustness of LLM judges and verifiers.} LLM judges are biased and manipulable
\citep{zheng2023judging}. For math verification, \citet{huang2025accuracy} show that model-based verifiers improve
recall over rules but accept hacking patterns, and \citet{zhao2025onetoken} find that single tokens such as
punctuation or reasoning openers fool many judges; discriminative verifiers such as xVerify are far more robust
\citep{chen2025xverify}. These works measure what a judge accepts; we ask which of those flaws training finds, and
show that static accuracy and FPR alone are weaker predictors than exposure.

\paragraph{When reward hacking emerges.} CHERRL injects known biases into rubric judges and studies their
discoverability and exploitability during training \citep{wang2026rubric}. Countdown-Code shows that 1\% of hacking
trajectories in distillation data makes hacking resurface in RL \citep{khalifa2026countdown}, and CATCH shows, for
coding RL with designed-in loopholes, that a model's initial tendency to hack and the difficulty of earning reward
both shape when hacking emerges \citep{wang2026catch}; our H2 and H3 are their counterparts for real judges. Other
work predicts hacking from fine-tuning without RL \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}, measures exploits by agents
\citep{thaman2026benchmark} or in rubric RL \citep{mahmoud2026rubric}, and tests verifiers by fuzzing
\citep{ray2026fuzzing} or with injected noise \citep{plesner2026imperfect}. We differ in measuring strictly before
RL, in targeting the natural flaws of real judges, in predicting which of many competing loopholes wins, and in
testing on held-out judges with propensity under experimental control.

% ============================================================================ 7 conclusion
\section{Conclusion}

Which loophole of an LLM judge a policy exploits is predictable before training: it is the pattern with the highest
exposure, the product of how often the policy produces it and how often the judge accepts it, and difficulty
decides whether any pattern is reached at all. A judge's static accuracy, or its FPR alone, is not enough to screen
it as a reward; an audit of a few GPU hours that also measures the starting policy can flag a risky pairing before a
run that costs hundreds. Whether the same holds for natural propensities in larger policies is the next question.

% ============================================================================ limitations (required, unnumbered)
\section*{Limitations}

Our evidence comes from one 1.5B policy on integer-answer math, 14 known pattern families and judges that fit beside
training on one GPU, with a 300-step budget; larger policies may find patterns outside the families we detect.
Natural propensities were too low for any pattern to be found at this scale, so propensity was raised by controlled
seeding; this models fine-tuning data that contain such responses, not their spontaneous emergence. Events are few
(14 in 12 runs) and the held-out judges are three, so per-judge intervals are wide and transfer is a limited check.
The dose test (H3) did not reach significance, and the hard and easy pools differ in problem source as well as
difficulty. These limits bound the generality of the claim; they do not affect its internal validity, since every
prediction was recorded before its run and the analysis was fixed in advance.

\section*{Ethics statement}

The hacking patterns studied here were published before this work \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}. Our
results show which of them small policies exploit; this helps practitioners screen judges before RL and helps
evaluators who rely on LLM judges, but could also help someone game a judge-based evaluation. We report every
pattern and judge so that these weaknesses are known and testable. All data and models are used under their licenses
for research; xVerify-0.5B-I is licensed for non-commercial use and was used for inference only. The experiments
used about 212 GPU hours.

\section{Statistical details and sensitivity analyses}
\label{app:stats}

\paragraph{H1.} AUROC counts ties as one half. Each of 10,000 bootstrap replicates resamples whole runs with
replacement within each judge; replicates without an event are dropped (1 of 10,000 on the core runs). Differences to
each baseline use the same replicates and units, with the two-sided $p$-value $2\min(P(d \le 0), P(d \ge 0))$, and a
$p$ of 0 enters the Holm correction as $10^{-4}$.

\paragraph{H2 and H3.} H2 is the exact McNemar test on the 12 pairs of core runs that share judge, checkpoint and seed,
with ``some family has an event'' as the outcome of a run. For H3 the statistic is the sum over all core events of
the rank (1 to 3) of the dose its family received; its null distribution is taken over all 252,252 ways of splitting
the 14 families into groups of five, five and four with outcomes held fixed, which is exact under the null hypothesis
that the dose changes no outcome, competition within a run included. The observed statistic, 25, is the second
largest the design allows (one-sided $P = 0.033$, two-sided $0.056$; the two-sided test was specified).

\paragraph{Secondary analyses.} Log models use the Jeffreys-smoothed FPR $(k + 0.5)/(n + 1)$, since many FPRs are 0.
Each family is censored at the first event of another family in its run (cause-specific hazards); a Cox model was
fitted only where there are at least 10 events per covariate, which holds for all 33 seeded runs (12 events) and not
for the core runs alone (7). The winner conditional logit separates the data for the full and the product model, so
their likelihoods are suprema. Early growth is the change in the log share (with 0.5 added) of a family among the
training responses between steps 0 to 9 and the 10 steps ending 10 steps before the run's first event (steps 40 to 49
without an event).

\paragraph{Sensitivity analyses.} Each variant changes one part of the primary test and repeats it on the core runs
(Table~\ref{tab:sensitivity}). The event definition varies $\tau$ over half, once and twice the frozen value, each with
one or two consecutive evaluations above the step-0 value; the looser definitions add single-evaluation rises of 5 to
13 outputs in 200. Propensity is replaced by the total log-likelihood of the family's canonical strings under the
fine-tuned policy, combined with FPR in log space, or by their per-token log-likelihood; FPR is replaced by the stage-1
mean over each family's strings in the hacking set, which leaves out Qwen3.6, not audited in stage 1. The lead of
exposure over FPR alone stays between 0.037 and 0.041 except for per-token likelihood, which favors short repetitive
strings and is not a frequency. None of these variants enters the Holm family.
