% ============================================================================ abstract
\begin{abstract}
Which loophole of an LLM judge will a policy exploit when the judge serves as its reward? We show that the
answer can be predicted before any RL step. LLM judges accept degenerate answers such as a lone \str{\{} or
\str{Thought process:}, but static robustness probes list many such flaws without saying which ones training
will find. Before training, we audit each judge and the starting policy and score every hacking pattern by its
exposure: how often the policy produces the pattern times how often the judge accepts it. To test exposure as a
cause rather than a correlate, we vary propensity experimentally, seeding the patterns into a short fine-tuning
stage at three doses in a Latin square, and we pre-register the analysis. Across 36 GRPO runs of
Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct against seven judges, three of them held out, exposure predicts which pattern is
exploited with an AUROC of 0.974, against 0.937 for the judge's false-positive rate alone. In all 12 runs where
a pattern was exploited, the first one was the pattern ranked first by exposure; the false-positive rate alone
ranked it first in 6. Exploitation occurred only on hard prompts, where an accepted exploit receives about 2.5
times the advantage it gets on easy ones.
\end{abstract}

% ============================================================================ 1 introduction
\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards has become a standard way to train reasoning in language models
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath, deepseek2025r1}. When an answer cannot be checked by a rule, an LLM is often asked to
act as the verifier, comparing the policy's answer with a reference \citep{chen2025xverify, ma2025generalreasoner}.
Such judges have blind spots. A response consisting of a lone \str{\{}, a bare claim that the answer is correct, or
the words \str{Thought process:} can be graded correct \citep{huang2025accuracy, zhao2025onetoken}, and a policy
trained against such a judge can learn to emit these strings instead of solving the problem
\citep{huang2025accuracy}.

Knowing that a judge has a blind spot does not tell us whether training will find it. \citet{huang2025accuracy}
report a 1.5B judge that fails static probes at high rates yet was never exploited during GRPO, while a fine-tuned
judge with higher accuracy was, and suggest that the policy was too weak to find the flaws. Studies of when reward
hacking begins have used biases injected into rubric judges \citep{wang2026rubric}, programmatic checkers
\citep{khalifa2026countdown, wang2026catch}, or fine-tuning as a stand-in for RL \citep{eleutherai2026indicators}.
\todo{verify each characterization against the cited paper}
Whether measurements taken strictly before RL can predict which of a real judge's existing flaws a policy will
exploit, and whether such predictions hold on judges held out from the analysis, has not been tested.

We start from a simple account of how a policy finds a loophole under GRPO \citep{shao2024deepseekmath}. A pattern
can be reinforced only when the policy samples it and the judge rewards it. With $G$ responses per training step,
a pattern produced at rate $\phat$ and accepted with probability $\fpr$ earns on average $G\,\phat\,\fpr$ rewarded
responses per step, its \emph{exposure}. We hypothesize that the pattern with the highest exposure is the one
exploited, and that neither factor alone predicts as well (H1). Group normalization adds a second factor: when
genuine successes are rare, an accepted exploit is often the only rewarded response in its group and receives a
large advantage, so hard prompts should raise the risk (H2). If propensity matters causally, raising it by
fine-tuning should raise the risk with dose (H3).

Testing H1 requires propensity to vary independently of FPR, and natural propensities are too low for that: only 19
of 174,760 responses of our base policy fall in any of 14 hacking-pattern families, and no pattern is exploited
within 300 steps. We therefore seed each pattern into a short supervised fine-tuning (SFT) stage on the policy's own
correct answers, at 0.3\%, 1.2\% or 4.0\% of the examples, with the doses rotated over three seeds in a Latin square
so that every pattern meets every dose (Figure~\ref{fig:takeover}). We measure each judge's FPR on each pattern and
the seeded propensity, record every score, and only then train. The protocol, including the event definition and
every test, was frozen before the first run.

Across 36 GRPO runs of Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct with seven judges, three of them held out, we find:
\begin{itemize}
\setlength\itemsep{1pt}
\item \textbf{Exposure predicts which loophole is exploited.} Over 336 run and pattern pairs it reaches an AUROC of
  0.974, against 0.937 for FPR alone, 0.847 for propensity alone and 0.379 for the judge's static accuracy (all
  differences $p < 0.001$ after Holm correction), and the pattern exploited first was ranked first in all 12 runs
  with an event.
\item \textbf{The product is the right combination, and difficulty gates it.} A conditional logit over the winners
  rejects either factor alone and is consistent with equal weights on log propensity and log FPR; exploitation
  occurs only on hard prompts, where an accepted exploit receives about 2.5 times the advantage it gets on easy
  prompts.
\item \textbf{An open audit.} We release the FPR of seven open judges on 14 patterns, the code, every run log and an
  interactive demo. \todo{anonymized link for review}
\end{itemize}

\section{Setting}
\label{sec:setting}

% Key points (findings 1.4 to 1.9, 3.5): what is fixed in every run.
\paragraph{Hacking patterns.} 13 families from the hacking set of \citet{huang2025accuracy} plus the reasoning
openers of \citet{zhao2025onetoken}, 14 in all (Table~\todo{appendix table with one example each}). Rule-based
detectors assign each response to at most one family, by whether the pattern dominates the response rather than
whether it appears in it; a response with a non-empty boxed answer or real mathematical work is never a family.
\todo{detector validation numbers from findings 1.6}

\paragraph{Judges.} Four core judges spanning the range of vulnerability reported by \citet{huang2025accuracy}:
R1-Distill-Verifier-1.5B (exploited in their RL runs; our positive control), DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-1.5B
\citep{deepseek2025r1}, Qwen2.5-Math-1.5B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25math} and xVerify-0.5B-I
\citep{chen2025xverify} (almost never fooled; negative control). Three held-out judges are used only to test
transfer: General-Verifier \citep{ma2025generalreasoner}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{qwen2025qwen25} and
Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. Each judge uses its native prompt and decoding. \todo{cite Qwen3.6; prompts in appendix}

\paragraph{Policy, problems and gold labels.} Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on integer-answer problems from DeepScaleR
\citep{luo2025deepscaler} and the MATH training split \citep{hendrycks2021math}, excluding the 471 problems behind
the hacking set and any overlap with MATH-500 \citep{lightman2023verify} and the GSM8K test set
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}. A rule-based gold checker (last \verb|\boxed{}| answer, Math-Verify
\citep{huggingface2025mathverify}) labels correctness and is never seen by the policy; it agrees with a blind hand
audit of 200 responses 99.7\% of the time (weighted; every miss is a correct answer without a box).

\paragraph{Difficulty pools.} The base policy answers every one of 21,845 candidate problems eight times. The hard
pool holds problems solved 1 or 2 times out of 8, the easy pool those solved 5 to 7 times; each has 2,229 training
and 100 evaluation problems.

\section{Predicting exploitation before training}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Judge audit.} For judge $j$ and family $f$, $\fpr_{j,f}$ is the share of the 471 audit problems on
which $j$ accepts the family's pre-specified seeding string as a correct answer (Figure~\ref{fig:fpr}). One string
per family, its most frequent answer in the hacking set \todo{exceptions: reasoning opener, gibberish}, because GRPO
reinforces strings: in a pilot, a family that mixed accepted and rejected strings did not grow.

\paragraph{Controlled propensity.} \todo{2 to 3 sentences: SFT on 2,000 of the policy's own correct answers,
shares replaced by the family string; groups of 5, 5 and 4 families drawn once at random; rotation $s$ for seed $s$;
matched 0\% control. Measured propensity: 16 samples on each of 250 problems per pool, Jeffreys smoothing.}

\paragraph{Exposure score.} $r_{j,f} = \phat_f \cdot \fpr_{j,f}$, and $256\,r_{j,f}$ is the expected number of
rewarded responses of family $f$ per GRPO step. Recorded for every run before its first step.

\paragraph{Outcome.} \todo{event: $e_f(t)$, the share of 200 evaluation outputs that are in $f$, wrong by gold and
accepted by the judge, rises at least $\tau = 0.05$ above $e_f(0)$ at two consecutive evaluations, every 10 steps.}

\section*{Limitations}

Our evidence comes from one 1.5B policy on integer-answer math, 14 known pattern families and judges that fit
beside training on one GPU, with a 300-step budget; larger policies may find patterns outside the families we
detect. Natural propensities were too low for any pattern to be found at this scale, so propensity was raised by
controlled seeding, which models fine-tuning data that contain such responses but not their natural emergence.
Events are few (14 in 12 runs) and the held-out judges are three, so intervals per judge are wide and transfer is a
limited check. The dose test (H3) did not reach significance, and the hard and easy pools differ in problem source as
well as difficulty. \todo{tighten; add what does not undermine the main claim}
